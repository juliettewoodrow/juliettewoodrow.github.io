Lecture 1 · 25 minutes

Linear regression: a map inside GPT-2

GPT-2 turns the word "Chicago" into 768 numbers. Can a straight line turn those numbers back into a latitude and longitude? We learn linear regression on 1.4 million real New York taxi rides, then point it at the inside of a language model.

  • Predict with a weighted sum of features. The coefficients are the model.
  • Score a line by its mean squared error (ordinary least squares).
  • Learn the coefficients two ways: gradient descent, or solve directly.
  • Measure R² on rides the model never saw.
  • Fit two regressions on GPT-2's internal representations of 24,665 cities and test on 6,118 held-out cities.

Lecture 2 · 25 minutes

Inside a large language model

In 2022, researchers rewrote a fact inside GPT so it would say the Space Needle is in Paris. Where could a fact live inside a network? We open GPT-2 up, part by part, until the question has an answer.

  • From ten digit scores to 50,257 token scores: softmax gives every next token a probability.
  • Word embeddings are learned parameters, and similar words get similar parameters.
  • Attention lets a word read the words before it; learned weights decide how much from each.
  • GPT-2 running live in the browser, writing one token at a time.

1. Why not just ask GPT-2 where a city is?slides 1 to 3

If we want to know whether GPT-2 knows where Cambridge is, why not ask it?

We did. Prompted with "The latitude and longitude of Cambridge, Massachusetts is", GPT-2 small writes "about 1,000 miles (1,200 km) from" and never produces a coordinate. What a model is willing to say is not the same as what it represents. So the experiment asks a different question: when the model merely reads a city's name, is its location recoverable from the numbers inside, even though nobody asked?

2. More cities through the probeslides 4 to 11

In the lecture we tried Chicago. Here the same two regression models (one for latitude, one for longitude) get New York City, Oslo, Reykjavík, Tehran and Cambridge, Massachusetts, none of which were used to fit the coefficients. Tehran lands 70 km off. New York lands in the Atlantic, 880 km off. Cambridge lands in Kansas, 2,230 km off: GPT-2 small does not know where MIT is.

The section ends with the debate these results feed: is a map "in there", or is this parroting? Being able to decode a quantity with a straight line shows the information is present. It does not show the model uses it.

3. Why the squared vertical error?slides 12 to 15

Why square the errors instead of adding them? Why measure vertically and not perpendicular to the line?

Adding signed errors over all 1,154,203 training trips gives exactly zero for the line through the mean: plus 3.18 million minutes above, minus 3.18 million below. The sum measures the balance of over- and under-prediction, not closeness. Squaring stops the cancellation and weights a 10-minute miss 25 times more than a 2-minute miss. Absolute error is a valid alternative (it gives a median-style fit). Squared error is also the maximum-likelihood choice when the noise around the line is Gaussian.

Vertical, because the task is to predict a duration given a distance. The vertical gap is the number of minutes our prediction missed by. Perpendicular distance (total least squares) answers a different question, useful when both coordinates are measured with error.

4. From maximum likelihood to gradient descentslides 16 to 20

The review I cut for time. Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the data most likely. With one parameter (seven heads in ten flips) you can find the top of the log-likelihood by gradient ascent: find the slope, step uphill, repeat, and stop at p = 0.7. Machine learning usually has a loss we want small instead, so we flip the sign and descend. Gradient descent is the same one move, downhill.

The last slide is the picture I like best: climbing a real mountain in the fog (the Island of Hawai'i, from a real elevation model) when all you can read is the altimeter under your feet.

5. The derivation, step by stepslides 21 to 30

Everything the lecture compressed into two slides. The goal: find β₀, β₁ that minimise L(β₀, β₁) = (1/n) Σ [yᵢ − (β₀ + β₁xᵢ)]². At the lowest point of the loss surface both slopes are zero. Step one, the chain rule applied to one trip and then summed, gives ∂L/∂β₀ = −(2/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ) and ∂L/∂β₁ = −(2/n) Σ xᵢ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ). Step two, two ways: walk downhill, or set both to zero and solve.

Solving: the first equation gives β₀ = ȳ − β₁x̄ (so the fitted line passes through the point of means). Substituting into the second gives β₁ = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / Σ(xᵢ − x̄)², which needs some variation in x. For the taxi data: 6.56 minutes plus 3.47 minutes per mile. The coefficients are the model; everything a model knows lives in them, two numbers here and 124 million in GPT-2.

6. Gradient descent in code, and when the formula failsslides 31 to 35

The whole walk is a dozen lines of Python: start at the flat line, compute the two slopes, nudge each coefficient against its slope, repeat 80 times. Why would anyone walk when there is a formula? Because the walk only needs slopes. It works for any model you can differentiate, including a neural network, for which there is no formula for the bottom.

The matrix form β* = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy handles any number of inputs in one line. It fails when the columns of X are not independent (square feet and square metres as two inputs, or more inputs than examples), and it becomes impractical when XᵀX is simply too large to form. In both cases, iterative methods still work.

7. Reading R² honestlyslides 36 to 41

R² = 0.75 does not mean 75% of trips were predicted correctly.

It means the model eliminated 75% of the squared error that predicting the mean would leave. R² = 0 is no better than the mean; R² = 1 is a perfect fit; R² can be negative on new data (never on the data the line was fit to). People use it because it has no units, so the taxi model and the GPT-2 probe can be compared on one scale. That is also its weakness, so the slides add RMSE and MAE in minutes: knowing the distance cuts the typical miss from 10.8 to 6.9 minutes.

The honest score is on held-out data. Adding features can only raise training R²; test R² can fall. The gap between them is overfitting.

8. More features: rush hour and airportsslides 42 to 50

Add a rush-hour flag and the model becomes two parallel lines, 2.2 minutes apart. Add a distance × rush-hour term and the lines can have different slopes: rush hour turns out not to be a fixed delay but a 39% slowdown per mile. Held-out R² climbs from 0.60 to 0.62.

Airport trips average 35 minutes, three times a normal ride. Why does the airport feature get a negative coefficient (−2.1 minutes)?

Because the model already knows the distance. Given the distance, airport trips are faster: highways, about 4.2 minutes per mile against 7.3 elsewhere. A coefficient is the effect of a feature holding the others fixed. Read every coefficient that way. The section closes with the matrix form of the normal equations for any number of features.

9. Inside GPT-2, in more detailslides 51 to 59

The GPT-2 experiment written out in the lecture's own notation: two regressions with 769 coefficients each (768 features plus an intercept), fit by gradient descent on 24,665 training cities, with one partial derivative per coefficient and the same update rule as the taxi model. Then the learned coefficients applied to Chicago, number by number, and the held-out map with its two R² scores (0.56 for latitude, 0.70 for longitude).

Finally, a bigger model. The same recipe on Llama 3.1 (8 billion parameters) gives a mean R² of 0.93 against 0.63 for GPT-2 small. Nobody asked either model to build a map.
